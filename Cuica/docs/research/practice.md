# Cuica playing practice (read directly, 2026-10-28)

- GALANTE = Rafael B. F. Galante, "Da cupópia da cuíca", MA dissertation in social history, USP 2015 (teses.usp.br tde-02062015-175712). History; its music transcriptions are images (not extracted).

## Cloth grip changes pitch (not only loudness)
- "A pressão usada na fricção do gambito também interfere na altura, quanto maior a pressão mais grave o som" (more cloth pressure = LOWER pitch) — Mestrinel 2009 p.103, via BICOLOR p.46.
- Galante's transcription notation: "x" under a note = the rubbing hand REDUCING cloth pressure on the rod to change the note (GALANTE p.90, 1936 recording "Deixa a cuíca roncá").
- Pitch changed "both by pressing the fingers of the left hand against the head and by varying the pressure on the stroking cloth" — Reed & Leach 1978 p.89; "any slight variation of pressure on the rods can change the pitch" — Gianesella 2009 p.133 (BICOLOR p.46).
- Before the 1930s the cuica/puíta was played with the rubbing hand only (no head pressing); notes came from the friction alone (GALANTE p.84 photo caption; Amaral 2010 p.56 via BICOLOR p.44). Head-pressing technique is a Rio invention from ~1930s on.
- Rubbing nearer the head (centre end of the rod) = higher (Farias 2010 p.92, BICOLOR p.49).
- Inference: matches the bowed-string "flattening effect" (more normal force lowers pitch) — see research2_bowed.md. Our Pressure knob currently does not move pitch.

## Stroke technique
- Two basic strokes, pull and push; rhythm articulated by pushing and pulling along the rod (Miller 2009 p.164; Uribe 2006 p.52).
- Sabanovich 2012 p.26: minimum cloth pressure needed; "for more volume use a longer stroke with more speed, not more pressure"; "with faster tempos the strokes get shorter"; "try to get the same sound in both directions" (supports Pull 0). Cloth kept moist with water, kerosene or milk.
- Chalo & Kumor 2001 p.45: louder/"more curious" sound by slightly more pressure on the cloth with thumb and index; the rod is not pulled (it would break).
- Cloth: grosgrain ("gorgorão") ribbon, wetted with water, alcohol or kerosene (kerosene "gives more grip") — Farias 2010, Mestrinel 2009, Marcon 1997.
- Rubbing with a wet hand, leather, sponge, or rosin are documented alternatives (BICOLOR Quadro 14).

## Head pressing
- Pressing raises pitch; harder = higher; nearer the centre/"bead" (the knot of the rod) = higher (Sabanovich; Jenkins 2009 p.47; Ribeiro 2005).
- Sabanovich: THREE discrete pitch levels from finger combinations near the bead on large cuicas: low = ring finger only; middle = index + ring; high = all three. Small cuicas: thumb only, press-and-release. "Alternating finger pressure on and off, combined with the push/pull, the rhythmic variations are immense."
- "Low at the fingertip, high at the fingernail" (Lima 1988 p.143).
- Glissando during friction is possible because the rod is inside (Frungillo 2003 p.90); first heard on record in 1940 ("Iaiá ioiô e a cuíca"), later considered characteristic (GALANTE p.91).

## Range and register
- Range claims: over an octave (Galm & Galm 2014), ~2 octaves (Uribe 2006), ~3 octaves (Reed & Leach 1978) — none measured.
- "Rubbed to produce various partials of the harmonic series" (Galm 1977 p.189) — i.e. players can jump between regimes/harmonics.
- Usually written and played as a two-tone instrument, low and high (Netto 2003 p.20); "semi-melodic, few notes, high register, glissando" (Carvalho 1994).
- Carioca style is tuned very high to project outdoors (Chalo & Kumor 2001); 1930s cuicas were tuned much lower (one 1934 record near the guitar's low C, GALANTE p.82); "mooed like an ox" (Noel Rosa, 1935) vs today's high squeal.

## Sound descriptions (consensus words)
- "ronco" (snore/grunt) is the most frequent descriptor; "rouco" (hoarse); "ora esganiçado, ora rouco" (now shrill, now hoarse); grunts, moans, laughs, sobs, squeaks; a dog's howl to a pig's grunt; "with large dynamic range" (Statsenko 2015). A composer: "nothing is worse than a hoarse cuica" (wants bright and clear) — so hoarseness is a player/style choice.

## Patterns (GALANTE pp.89-93, transcriptions are images; described in text)
- 1930s: "marcação" (timekeeping) role, low tuning.
- From ~1939-1940s: solo cells built from LOW/HIGH alternation; Badu 1941 inverts the low/high of the candomblé "cabula" time-line; 1942: a constant cell on beats 1-2, variations on 3-4.
